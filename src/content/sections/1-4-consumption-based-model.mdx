## Paying for What You Use

Imagine you need electricity for your home. You have two choices. You could **buy a generator** — spend thousands of dollars upfront on the equipment, pay for fuel and maintenance, and be responsible for repairs when something breaks. You own the generator regardless of how much electricity you actually use; it costs the same whether you run it 24 hours a day or one hour a week. Or, you could simply **plug into the power grid** and pay the utility company a monthly bill based on exactly how many kilowatt-hours you consumed. No upfront investment, no maintenance, no waste — you pay only for what you use.

Cloud computing works like the power grid. Instead of buying servers, building data centers, and hiring staff to maintain them, organizations use a cloud provider like Azure and pay only for the computing resources they actually consume. This is the **consumption-based model**, and understanding the financial shift it represents — from capital expenditure to operational expenditure — is a core topic on the AZ-900 exam.

## Capital Expenditure (CapEx)

<KeyTerm term="Capital expenditure (CapEx)">An upfront investment in physical infrastructure — such as servers, data centers, networking equipment, and cooling systems — that is owned by the organization and depreciates in value over time.</KeyTerm>

In a traditional IT model, organizations spend large amounts of money upfront to build and equip their own data centers. This includes purchasing servers, storage arrays, networking hardware, backup power systems, cooling infrastructure, and the physical building itself. These purchases are capital expenditures — they appear on the balance sheet as assets and are depreciated over their useful life (typically 3-5 years for IT equipment).

**Characteristics of CapEx:**

- **Large upfront cost** — You pay for hardware before you use it. A single enterprise server can cost tens of thousands of dollars; a data center buildout can run into millions.
- **Asset depreciation** — The equipment loses value over time. A server purchased today is worth less next year and may be obsolete in five years.
- **Capacity planning required** — You must estimate future demand and buy enough hardware to handle it. Overestimate, and you waste money on idle equipment. Underestimate, and you can't serve your users.
- **Ongoing maintenance costs** — Even after the initial purchase, you pay for power, cooling, physical security, hardware repairs, and staff to manage it all.
- **Long procurement cycles** — Ordering, shipping, racking, and configuring new servers can take weeks or months.

Think of CapEx like buying a car. You pay a large sum upfront (or finance it), and then the car depreciates every year. Whether you drive 100 miles a month or 3,000 miles a month, your car payment stays the same. You're responsible for insurance, maintenance, fuel, and eventually replacing it when it wears out.

<Callout type="info">
CapEx isn't inherently bad — it makes sense when workloads are predictable and stable over long periods. Organizations with constant, well-understood compute needs may find that owning infrastructure is cost-effective over a multi-year horizon. The challenge arises when demand is variable, growth is uncertain, or technology changes rapidly.
</Callout>

## Operational Expenditure (OpEx)

<KeyTerm term="Operational expenditure (OpEx)">Ongoing spending on services and products as you use them, with no upfront investment in physical assets. Cloud computing costs are classified as OpEx because you pay a recurring fee based on consumption rather than purchasing infrastructure.</KeyTerm>

In a cloud model, there is no large upfront investment. Instead of buying servers, you rent computing capacity from a provider like Azure and pay a monthly (or even per-second) bill based on what you consumed. This spending is classified as operational expenditure — it's a regular, recurring business expense, like paying for electricity, office supplies, or employee salaries.

**Characteristics of OpEx:**

- **No upfront cost** — You start using cloud resources immediately without purchasing hardware. There's no capital investment to justify or depreciate.
- **Pay-as-you-go** — You're billed based on actual usage. Run a virtual machine for two hours? Pay for two hours. Shut it down? Stop paying.
- **Predictable and manageable** — While individual resource costs vary with usage, tools like Azure Cost Management help organizations set budgets, track spending, and forecast future costs.
- **No wasted capacity** — You don't pay for resources you don't use. Scale up when demand increases, scale down when it drops.
- **Immediate deduction** — OpEx is fully deductible in the fiscal year it's incurred, unlike CapEx which must be depreciated over multiple years.

Think of OpEx like using a rideshare service. You don't buy a car — you open an app, request a ride, and pay only for the trip. Short trip, small bill. Long trip, bigger bill. No trip, no cost. You never worry about depreciation, maintenance, or parking.

<Callout type="tip">
For the AZ-900 exam, remember this key distinction: CapEx is spending money upfront to buy physical infrastructure that depreciates over time. OpEx is spending money on services as you use them, with no upfront investment. Cloud computing shifts IT spending from CapEx to OpEx.
</Callout>

## The Consumption-Based Model

<KeyTerm term="Consumption-based model">A pricing model where you pay only for the cloud resources you actually use, rather than paying for fixed capacity. This is the fundamental pricing model of cloud computing and is the mechanism through which cloud shifts IT spending from CapEx to OpEx.</KeyTerm>

The consumption-based model is the financial engine of cloud computing. Rather than buying a fixed amount of computing capacity and hoping you estimated correctly, you consume resources on demand and pay based on what you actually used. Azure meters your usage — compute time, storage volume, data transfer, API calls — and bills you accordingly.

**Key benefits of the consumption-based model:**

- **No upfront costs** — Start building immediately without purchasing hardware or signing long-term contracts for infrastructure.
- **Scale with demand** — Provision more resources when traffic spikes; release them when demand drops. Your costs scale with your actual usage, not your estimated peak.
- **Stop paying when you stop using** — Deallocate a virtual machine and the compute charges stop. Delete a storage account and the storage charges stop. You're never stuck paying for resources you no longer need.
- **No wasted capacity** — Traditional IT forces organizations to buy enough hardware for peak demand, which means most of the time that hardware sits partially idle. The consumption-based model eliminates this waste.
- **Faster experimentation** — Want to test a new architecture? Spin up resources, run the experiment, and tear them down. You pay only for the time those resources existed.

## How Azure Pricing Works

<KeyTerm term="Pay-as-you-go pricing">Azure's default pricing model where you are billed based on actual resource consumption — per second of compute time, per gigabyte of storage, per gigabyte of network egress, per API transaction, and so on — with no minimum commitment.</KeyTerm>

Azure measures resource consumption at a granular level and bills accordingly. Here's how pricing works for common resource types:

- **Compute (Virtual Machines)** — Billed per second when the VM is running. A VM that runs for 3 hours costs exactly 3 hours of compute time. Deallocate (stop) the VM, and compute charges stop (though storage charges for the VM's disk continue).
- **Storage** — Billed per gigabyte per month. Store 100 GB for a month, pay for 100 GB. Delete 50 GB halfway through the month, and your bill adjusts.
- **Network egress** — Data leaving Azure (outbound) is metered and billed per gigabyte. Data entering Azure (inbound) is free. Transferring data between Azure services in the same region is typically free or low-cost.
- **Managed services (PaaS/SaaS)** — Services like Azure SQL Database, Azure Functions, and Azure App Service have their own pricing tiers, often based on a combination of compute units, storage, and throughput.

<Callout type="warning">
Not everything in Azure stops costing money when you stop using it. A deallocated virtual machine stops incurring compute charges, but you still pay for attached disk storage, reserved IP addresses, and any other persistent resources. Always review what you're being billed for using Azure Cost Management to avoid surprise charges.
</Callout>

<KeyTerm term="Azure Cost Management">A built-in Azure tool that helps organizations monitor, allocate, and optimize cloud spending. It provides cost analysis dashboards, budget alerts, and recommendations for reducing waste.</KeyTerm>

## Comparing CapEx and OpEx

| Dimension | CapEx (Traditional IT) | OpEx (Cloud / Consumption-Based) |
|---|---|---|
| **Upfront cost** | High — purchase hardware before use | None — start immediately |
| **Ongoing cost** | Maintenance, power, cooling, staff | Pay-per-use charges |
| **Scaling** | Buy more hardware (weeks/months) | Provision instantly, release when done |
| **Capacity planning** | Must estimate future demand | Scale on demand, no guessing |
| **Wasted resources** | Idle hardware when demand is low | Pay only for what you use |
| **Financial treatment** | Depreciated asset over 3-5 years | Expense deducted in current period |
| **Risk** | Hardware becomes obsolete | Provider keeps infrastructure current |
| **Speed** | Slow procurement cycles | Resources available in minutes |

<DeepDive title="Real-World Example: Startup vs. Enterprise — Why the Financial Model Matters">
Consider two companies launching the same web application.

**Company A (Traditional CapEx approach):** Before writing a single line of code, the company must estimate peak traffic, then purchase servers, storage, and networking equipment to handle that estimate plus a safety margin. They spend $200,000 on hardware, sign a colocation contract for rack space, and hire two systems administrators. Setup takes three months. On launch day, traffic is 20% of what they estimated — 80% of their capacity sits idle, but the bills keep coming. Six months later, a viral moment drives 5x their peak estimate, and the servers crash. They can't add capacity fast enough.

**Company B (Cloud OpEx approach):** The company signs up for Azure, deploys their application in an afternoon, and configures auto-scaling. On launch day, they pay for the small amount of compute and storage they actually use — maybe $500 for the first month. Six months later, when the viral moment hits, Azure automatically scales up to handle the spike. That month's bill jumps to $8,000, but the application stays online. When traffic normalizes, costs drop back down. No wasted capacity, no failed launch.

The consumption-based model didn't just save Company B money — it changed what was possible. They could launch faster, respond to demand instantly, and never risk paying for capacity they didn't need.
</DeepDive>

<DeepDive title="Azure Cost Management and Billing: Keeping Cloud Spending Under Control">
One common concern about the consumption-based model is unpredictability — if you pay for what you use, how do you keep costs from spiraling? Azure provides several tools to address this:

**Azure Cost Management + Billing** is a free, built-in tool that provides real-time cost tracking, historical analysis, and spending forecasts. You can see exactly what each resource costs, break down spending by department or project using tags, and identify underutilized resources.

**Budgets and alerts** let you set monthly or quarterly spending limits and receive email or SMS notifications when spending approaches or exceeds those thresholds. This prevents surprise bills.

**Azure Advisor cost recommendations** analyzes your resource usage and suggests optimizations — like downsizing underutilized VMs, deleting unused resources, or purchasing Reserved Instances for predictable workloads.

**Reserved Instances and Savings Plans** let you commit to a 1- or 3-year term in exchange for significant discounts (up to 72% off pay-as-you-go prices). This introduces a small element of CapEx-like commitment but dramatically reduces costs for workloads you know will run continuously.

The consumption-based model doesn't mean costs are uncontrollable — it means costs are visible, granular, and adjustable in real time.
</DeepDive>

<KeyTakeaways>
- **Capital expenditure (CapEx)** is upfront spending on physical infrastructure (servers, data centers, networking) that depreciates over time — the traditional IT model.
- **Operational expenditure (OpEx)** is ongoing spending on services as you use them, with no upfront investment — the cloud computing model.
- The **consumption-based model** means you pay only for the cloud resources you actually use. Scale up when demand increases, scale down when it drops, and stop paying when you stop using.
- Cloud computing shifts IT spending from CapEx to OpEx, eliminating large upfront investments and the risk of wasted capacity from over-provisioning.
- Azure bills at a granular level — per second of compute, per GB of storage, per GB of network egress — so costs track actual usage.
- **Azure Cost Management** provides budgets, alerts, cost analysis, and optimization recommendations to keep cloud spending predictable and controlled.
- Deallocating a VM stops compute charges, but persistent resources like disks and IP addresses may continue to incur costs until deleted.
- The consumption-based model enables faster experimentation, eliminates long procurement cycles, and lets organizations respond to demand changes in real time.
</KeyTakeaways>
